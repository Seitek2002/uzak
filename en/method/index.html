How the model calculates years

In plain words and with formulas: where the numbers in your report come from and what the model doesn’t know.

Baseline curve

The forecast is based on the WHO 2023 life table for your country, one of 185 countries covered. For each age, the table gives the share of people who die within a year. On average worldwide, a 30-year-old man lives to 75 years and a woman to 80 years.

One bad year should not be stretched over a whole lifetime. For 35 countries where 2023 was a year of war, disaster or a marked decline, or where the curve by age is broken, the model uses 2019 instead. National statistics may differ: in Kyrgyzstan, for example, the National Statistical Committee’s table matches the WHO up to age 69 but shows much higher mortality after that, so a forecast based on it would be lower.

Mortality is falling worldwide. The model assumes a decline of 1% a year, so the average forecast is higher than in a single year’s table.

If you lived in another country or plan to move, the model blends two tables: at the start of the forecast the past country weighs as much as the future one, then its weight fades.

The risk of each answer

For most of the 34 factors, the basis is a meta-analysis or a large cohort study. These give a hazard ratio, which shows how many times higher all-cause mortality is with this answer than with the best one. For example, people who smoke 10 or more cigarettes a day have 2.8 times the risk of those who never smoked. Some numbers come directly from the papers, some are recalculated for our answer options, and where data are scarce they are estimated. The model has not been reviewed by a physician or an epidemiologist.

Comparison with your peers

The table already includes smokers, people with high blood pressure and everyone else. So the model compares you with the average person of your sex and age in your country. Smoking, weight, blood pressure, physical activity, diabetes, alcohol and roads come from WHO data by country. The other shares are estimates: their template comes from the 2023 STEPS survey and the census in Kyrgyzstan, where we started, and it is approximate for other countries.

If all your answers match the average peer, the forecast is close to the table, adjusted for falling mortality. The model treats the answer “I don’t know” as average for your peers.

How answers add up

Risks are multiplied, as in most medical calculators. But some factors measure the same thing: steps, exercise, strength and heart rate reflect fitness, while weight and blood pressure reflect strain on blood vessels. Within such a group, the strongest effect counts in full and the next ones at 50, 30, 20 and 10%.

The sum of habits passes through a soft cap so that twenty small effects cannot produce absurd numbers. Family history and diagnoses are added on top.

How risk turns into years

Yearly risk t years from now, when you are x years old:

μ = μcountry(x) × 0.99t × (HR / HRpeers)k(x) + λ

HR is the total of your answers, and k is the strength of relative risks. Until age 55, k equals 1, and by age 80 it drops by 50%: in old age, smoking and weight affect risk less. λ is the risk on the road, which does not grow with age. The forecast in the report is the expected lifespan of a person who has already reached your age.

Biological age and potential

Biological age is the age at which the average person in your country has the same yearly risk as you.

Potential is the forecast if each habit moves one realistic step. For a smoker that means quitting, for someone with a BMI of 33 a drop to 28, and for someone who walks 5,000 steps an increase to 8,000 or 10,000. Country, family history and diagnoses are not part of the potential.

What the model doesn’t know

  • Your medical history and medications. Lung and kidney disease are not included in the test, nor is the type of cancer.
  • How well studies done mostly in Europe and the US apply to other countries.
  • Exact shares for some questions, such as blood tests, relationships and sleep. They are estimated, not measured.
  • Cause and effect. Studies show associations, and some of these may be explained by other factors.

So the forecast for one person can be off by years. Look at which factors pull it down, and discuss them with a doctor. Read more about why this is not a diagnosis.
